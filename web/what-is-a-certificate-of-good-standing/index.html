What is a certificate of good standing? What it proves, what it costs, and how to order one

A certificate of good standing is a one-page document from the state office that holds your company's records, usually the Secretary of State. It says your corporation or LLC exists and is current with that office's filing requirements on the day it is issued. States call it a Certificate of Status, Certificate of Existence or Certificate of Good Standing; they are the same thing. You order it from the state where the company was formed. It costs from nothing (Colorado) to $50 for Delaware's short form, and several states issue it online in minutes, though New York still takes written requests only. Before you order, ask the person who wants it how recent it must be.

What it proves, and what it does not

Colorado's Secretary of State puts it plainly: good standing "means that statutory filing requirements have been met with our office." The office does not check what the filings say, and it does not regulate how the business operates (Colorado SOS, Certificate of Good Standing FAQ).

So the certificate proves three things: the entity exists, it is on the state's register, and it has filed what that office requires, such as the annual report. It does not prove your trade or city license is valid, that your taxes with other agencies are paid, or that the business is reputable. A sole proprietor with no state filing has no certificate to order, because there is no entity on the register.

How recent it has to be

The certificate itself usually has no expiry date. Colorado says a certificate "doesn't expire", but it stops being valid if the entity's status changes, and the person accepting it "may require a specific time frame", for example an issue date within 30 or 60 days.

Some deadlines are written into law. Florida requires a foreign LLC applying to do business there to deliver a certificate of existence from its home state "dated not more than 90 days before the delivery of the application" (Florida Statutes, section 605.0902(2)).

The rule that follows: find out the window first, then order. Order too early and a slipped deal means paying twice.

Worked example: a Delaware LLC registering in Florida

A two-person LLC formed in Delaware is opening a Florida office and must register there as a foreign LLC.

  1. Status first. They confirm on Delaware's entity search that the LLC is active and that this year's annual LLC tax is paid.
  2. Order. On 1 December 2026 they request a short form Certificate of Status through Delaware's Document Filing and Certificate Request Service: $50.
  3. Count the window. Florida needs the certificate dated not more than 90 days before the application arrives. Day 90 after 1 December 2026 is 1 March 2027.
  4. Set two dates. A target to deliver the Florida application by 15 January 2027, and a hard stop of 1 March 2027. Miss the hard stop and they order a new certificate for another $50.

A California company asked by its bank does the same: $5 for the bizfile certificate, plus the free FTB letter if the bank wants tax standing, ordered once the bank names its window.

If the state says you are not in good standing

A state will not issue the certificate while the status shows anything else. Colorado says so directly. The usual causes are a missed annual report or unpaid state franchise tax. Work through it in this order:

  1. Read the entity record. The state's business search shows the status and often the missing filing.
  2. File and pay what is overdue. Find your state's report date and late fee in annual report due dates by state.
  3. Move fast if a notice arrived. In Texas, the Comptroller gives at least 45 days after mailing a notice of pending forfeiture. A forfeited entity cannot sue or defend itself in a Texas court, and its officers and directors can become liable for its debts.
  4. Reinstate if the entity was dissolved or forfeited, then order a fresh certificate. One issued before the status changed no longer counts.

Ignore paid mailings that offer to send you a certificate for a large fee. Colorado notes that its own official certificate is free on its website.

When this page does not apply

  • You need to know whether a trade or city license is valid. That is a different record. See how to check if a business license is valid.
  • A license, not the company, has lapsed. Read what happens if a business license expires.
  • Your company is Australian. ASIC, not a state, holds the record; start with the ASIC annual review date.
  • The status problem involves a dispute, a sale or a dissolved entity. Talk to a business attorney or the state's filing office before you refile anything.

Questions

Does a certificate of good standing expire?

Usually not by its own terms. Colorado says its certificate does not expire, but it stops being valid if the entity's status changes, and the person accepting it may want one issued within, for example, 30 or 60 days. Some laws set the window: Florida requires a foreign LLC's home-state certificate to be dated no more than 90 days before its application arrives.

How much does a certificate of good standing cost?

It depends on the state. On 26 November 2026: Colorado free, California $5, Illinois $25 for a corporation or LLC, New York $25, Delaware $50 short form or $175 long form. Expediting costs extra.

Is the online status search the same as a certificate?

Not always. Delaware says its paid online status check will not generate an official certificate of good standing. Colorado's free PDF from the entity page is the official certificate. Ask the requester which they will accept.
