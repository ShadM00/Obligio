How to check if a business license is valid, and what to read on the result

To check if a business license is valid, look it up on the public search of the agency that issued it, not a third-party site: the state board for a trade or profession, the state revenue or licensing department for a general state license, and the city or county clerk for a local license. A valid license shows a status such as "Active" or "Current, Active", an expiry date after the work will finish, the right name and, for trades, the right classification. A Secretary of State "Active" entry is not a license at all; it only means the company exists. The tables below show which lookup answers which question, what each status word means, and five checks to make beyond the status line. Sources were read on 19 November 2026.

Which lookup answers which question

A business can hold several licenses from different agencies, and each agency only shows its own. Decide first which one you need to see.

The SBA puts the local part plainly: "Local governments determine registration, licensing, and permitting requirements" (SBA, Apply for licenses and permits).

Why "Active" at the Secretary of State is not enough

California's Secretary of State defines Active as an entity that "has filed its formation document in California and is authorized to carry out its business activities", and adds "subject to any other requirements imposed by law". The licenses are those other requirements. A company can be Active there and hold no contractor license, or an expired one. The reverse also matters: an entity shown as Suspended (by the Secretary of State for missing filings, or by the Franchise Tax Board for tax reasons) has lost its powers to do business in the state, and the CSLB will not renew a contractor license for a corporation that is suspended. Check both records when you are hiring a company rather than a person.

California's CSLB writes its status as a sentence. An expired record reads "This license is expired and not able to contract at this time", and a second "Additional Status" line can say what is still needed, such as workers' compensation, before it can renew.

Five checks beyond the status line

A contractor's record answers more than "valid or not". Before you sign, check these in order and stop at the first one that fails.

  1. Same business. The name and license number on the quote or truck match the record. If the company name differs, ask why. If they will not give a number, do not go further.
  2. Right work. The classification covers the job. The CSLB tells consumers to confirm the contractor "is licensed for the specific type of work". A general building license is not an electrical or roofing one in every state.
  3. Still valid when the job ends. The expiry date falls after your finish date. If it falls during the job, ask whether the renewal is in.
  4. Bond and insurance on file. CSLB's detail page lists the contractor's bond (surety, number, amount, effective date) and the workers' compensation insurer and policy dates, or an exemption. Washington's Verify tool shows whether there are lawsuits against the bond and whether the workers' comp account is active and paid up.
  5. History. Read the complaint, citation or violation section. It is incomplete by design: CSLB says its "complaint disclosure is restricted by law", only construction-related civil judgments reported to it are shown, and citations are shown for five years from compliance.

Two cautions on the data itself. The CSLB says "there may be relevant information that has not yet been entered" because of workload, and Washington L&I says its database is updated on weekdays and "portions may be incorrect or not current". For a large job, print or save the page with the date, and call the board if anything looks wrong.

Checking your own license after you renew

If the license is yours, the public record is how a customer, a general contractor or a permit counter will see you. Look yourself up about a week after you pay. Check that the expiry date has moved forward, the address is current, and the bond and insurance shown are the policies you hold now. If the old date still shows, call the board that day; "renewal in process" is a status you want to see, not assume. The renewal steps are in how to renew a business license, and the dates to set reminders for are in contractor license renewal dates and reminders.

When this page does not apply

  • You think someone is working unlicensed. Report it to the board that would license the work; most boards have a complaint form on the same site as the lookup.
  • Your own license has already expired. Stop licensed work and read what happens if a business license expires.
  • The license is outside the US. Use the issuing regulator's register. For an Australian company's registration, see the ASIC annual review date guide.
  • A contract dispute or a claim against a bond is already under way. Talk to the board and to a lawyer; the lookup only tells you where the bond is held.

Questions

Is a business license number on an invoice proof that it is valid?

No. A number can be old, belong to someone else or cover different work. Enter it on the issuing agency's own lookup and check the name, status, expiry date and classification shown there.

Does an active listing with the Secretary of State mean a business is licensed?

No. California's Secretary of State defines Active as an entity that has filed its formation document and is authorized to carry out business activities, subject to any other requirements imposed by law. Licenses come from licensing boards and local governments and are looked up separately.

What if the license shows as delinquent or expired?

The holder should not be doing the licensed work. Florida's DBPR defines delinquent as not having completed a timely renewal, and California's CSLB shows an expired license as not able to contract. Ask the holder for proof of renewal and recheck the lookup, or call the board.
